# v0.5 plan — Level 3, feature one: sealed content and permissions

**Written:** session 66 (2026-11-12), Fable 5.2, from a design round with Venkat.
**Status:** a design direction, agreed in the round and **not yet a locked decision**.
Nothing here is normative and nothing is built. **For:** Opus, to turn into RFC-6.
**Decision behind it:** #99 (version 0.5 is Level 3). **Research:** `plans/l3/` R1–R7,
reviewed this session (one correction, in R1's header).

## 0. Sequence

Level 3 arrives as two features, each with its own RFC.

1. **RFC-6, sealed content and permissions** (this plan). Encrypted content on the
   wire, and the way a reader gets a key: already holding it, asking for it, and being
   approved or refused by rule, by a person, or by a person's AI delegate. **No payment.**
2. **RFC-7, payments** (future, not started). Payment as one more ground on which a key
   is granted. §8 lists what RFC-6 must leave room for so that RFC-7 adds no new
   abstraction.

The path for RFC-6, per #66 and #90: Opus drafts `docs/rfcs/rfc-6-sealed-content.md`
from this plan → Fable edits and rules → it publishes on blyg.blygger.org with a comment
period on Venkat's word → at close Fable revises, rules, and promotes it as a technical
note → **then** the studio builds, and the 0.5 document opens when the first construct is
built and exercised (#21, #43). Nothing is built in the studio before the close, except
a throwaway probe if Venkat says so (§10).

## 1. The use case

Ordinary information sharing inside an organisation, **with no assumed structure**.
Anybody can publish to the eligible population, to any segment of it, and decide for each
segment who is pre-approved, who is approved or refused automatically, and what goes to a
person as an exception.

- The first user is the Protocol Institute, a decentralised organisation **without
  single sign-on**. Sign-on may be used where it exists; it must never be required.
- A traditional organisation with one shared set of permission groups and a decentralised
  one where every node keeps its own must both fit, without the protocol knowing which it
  is looking at.
- Requesting access across organisations and across different clients is in scope.
- Redaction is a main case: a phrase inside a sentence, sealed to a narrower group than
  the sentence around it.

## 2. Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sealed entry** | One piece of encrypted content in an item document: ciphertext plus the wrapped key(s) that open it. |
| **Cover** | The readable text that stands where sealed content is, written by the author ("the acquirer", "restricted"). It is ordinary published content. |
| **Entry key** | The key that decrypts one entry. Fresh whenever the sealed content changes, so it is per version. |
| **Lock** | An opaque random id naming a **lock key**. Holding the lock key is standing membership: it unwraps every entry key wrapped under that lock. Roughly a standing permission group, except that the member list is never on the wire. |
| **Home** | The URL where a lock's keys are asked for. Not necessarily on the publishing origin. |
| **Grant** | A key (a lock key or one entry key) wrapped to a recipient (an origin's public key, or another lock's key). |
| **Ladder** | The private rules at a lock's home that decide a request: pre-approved, automatic yes, automatic no, or ask a person or their delegate. |

Two grant sizes exist and the difference matters throughout: **the lock key** (standing
membership, past and future) and **one entry key** (a single exception).

## 3. What goes on the wire

Field names below are illustrative. The RFC proposes names; Fable rules them.

### 3.1 The `sealed` member and the cover

An item document gains one OPTIONAL member, a list of sealed entries. Level 2 readers
ignore it (§13.1) and read the cover, so every sealed item is still a valid document and
a valid feed entry (#4).

```json
"content_md": "There is an acquisition offer from the acquirer.",
"sealed": [
  { "entry": "e1",
    "wraps": [ { "lock": "k7f3…", "home": "https://keys.example/", "key": "<entry key wrapped under the lock key>" } ],
    "nonce": "…", "ct": "…" }
]
```

- **`content_md` carries only covers and clear text. No new markup.** This is the
  two-plane pattern of generated text (#20): marker-free source, a list in the item
  document, and a baked wrapper class in `content_html`.
- **`content_html` wraps each cover in a new permanent class**, working name
  `blyg-sealed`, with an attribute naming its entry. A reader holding the key replaces
  the cover with the decrypted content; everyone else shows the cover. The class is a
  wire token like `blyg-transclusion` and `blyg-tk-gen`.
- **`content_hash` stays what it is**: the hash of `content_md`, which is what a reader
  without a key reads.
- The studio's authoring mark for a sealed span is studio-private, as the generation
  grammar is, and never reaches the wire.

### 3.2 Three granularities, one mechanism

| Granularity | Shape |
|---|---|
| Whole item | A **sealed item**: the cover is the whole of the required content members; one entry holds the body. |
| A block or a phrase | A **sealed span** inside any item. Not an item: no id, no version, no feed event of its own. |
| A block that deserves its own identity | A sealed item, quoted with the existing `![[id]]` directive. |

A sealed item is the case where one span covers the whole body. There is no third kind
and no change to the kinds.

### 3.3 Nesting

An entry's plaintext is itself content and may carry sealed entries of its own, to any
depth, under any locks. The inner entries sit inside the outer ciphertext.

- **Nesting is containment (AND).** A reader needs the outer key to learn that an inner
  entry exists.
- **"More restrictive" is the author's intent, not something the protocol checks.**
  Locks are independent. A client SHOULD warn when an inner lock has members the outer
  one lacks.

### 3.4 Several wraps on one entry (OR)

An entry may carry its key wrapped under several locks. Any one opens it; a reader that
holds more than one uses any, and the choice is local and unobservable. So locks compose
by OR within an entry and by AND through nesting.

### 3.5 Quoting sealed content

- A thread that quotes a sealed item carries a sealed copy of the quoted version, tied to
  that quote, with the item's cover as the visible text. Snapshot independence (§10.4)
  is kept: the copy does not depend on the source afterwards.
- **The quoter needs no key.** The ciphertext and its wraps are copied as they stand.
  Redactions survive quoting, and key holders still see through.
- Quoting a sealed item into a thread for another audience does not widen who can read
  it. Audience travels with the lock, not with the context.
- A span is bound to its document. A passage that should be reusable is made an item.

### 3.6 Origin keys and grant files

- **An origin may publish one public key in its manifest.** It is the origin's, never an
  author's, and it is for encryption only (§7).
- **A grant is a static file** holding a key wrapped to its recipient, at a path only
  the granter and the recipient can compute (derived from a secret they share and the
  lock or entry id). The reader probes with an ordinary GET: present means granted.
  Nobody else can tell which paths exist for whom, and there is no listing.
- **Recipients are of two sorts**: an origin (its manifest key), or a lock (the grant is
  wrapped under that lock's key). The second is a **lock-to-lock grant**: every holder of
  lock A also reads everything under lock B. This is how an organisation expresses its
  own ordering ("the board reads whatever the C-suite reads") with no hierarchy on the
  wire.
- A refusal may be delivered the same way: a file at the same path holding a sealed note.

### 3.7 The request endpoint

A lock's home answers requests for keys. It is a second dynamic surface, OPTIONAL like
the Webmention receiver; a static-only publisher issues grants by hand or uses a home run
by someone else.

- **Plain HTTP, one URL per lock and one per entry.**
- **Three answers**: granted (the key, or a statement that a grant file now exists),
  pending (decide later; the requester polls the grant path), refused (optional note).
- A request names the requester's origin and may carry a note and any evidence the home
  accepts, a sign-on token among them. The home wraps any grant to the key in that
  origin's manifest, so a false claim of origin earns a file the claimant cannot open.
- What the ladder contains, who is on which roster, and how a person or delegate is
  consulted are private to the home.

## 4. How a reader gets content

1. **Discover.** The entry names its locks and their homes. A reader holding any of the
   lock keys unwraps locally: no request, no traffic, nothing logged. This covers nested
   entries whose locks it holds.
2. **Probe.** Holding none, it checks its grant paths at each home (it may have been
   granted the lock, or this one entry, or a lock it holds may have been granted this
   one).
3. **Ask.** Finding nothing, it may send a request, to whichever home it thinks likeliest
   to agree.
4. **Decide.** The home runs its ladder:
   - **pre-approved**: the grant already exists;
   - **automatic**: a rule over origins, rosters (an OPML list is one natural form) or
     presented evidence grants or refuses;
   - **exception**: queued for the lock's owner or their AI delegate, working from the
     owner's standing instructions.
5. **Receive.** A grant file appears, or a refusal, or nothing.

Rules for a delegate, which the RFC should state as client guidance:

- A requester's note is untrusted text and is handled as data.
- A delegate may grant entry keys only, never a lock key.
- An exception does not recurse: a key for an outer entry opens no inner one.

**Where the centralisation lives.** A traditional organisation homes common locks at one
place and every author seals under them (wrapping is symmetric, so any holder of a lock
key can seal under it from their own origin). A decentralised one homes locks per node,
each with its own rosters, which agree only where nodes choose to share one. The wire
says "this lock, ask here" in both cases.

## 5. Widening, and what revocation can mean

**Widening** (declassifying):

| Scope | How | Visible on the wire |
|---|---|---|
| One entry | A new version adding a wrap under the wider lock. Ciphertext unchanged, so it reads as maintenance | Yes: the entry now names two locks |
| One group includes another | A lock-to-lock grant | No |
| One pinned entry | A grant of that entry's key to a lock or origin; the pinned bytes are untouched | No |
| Public | Reveal the entry key in the clear (commit-and-reveal), or publish a new version with the words in place of the cover | Yes |

**Revocation**, stated honestly, in the register of §9:

- It stops future grants and, after a lock is rotated, future content. Nothing else.
- Nothing recalls a key or text a reader already has.
- A pinned version keeps the wraps it was pinned with, forever.
- Widening is one-way.

## 6. Kinds: two companion rulings from the round

These came up while settling blocks and stand apart from Level 3. **Recorded the same
session as decision #100**, in the 0.4 text's thirteenth revision (§5.3, §13.3, §13.7);
reasoning in `v0.4-plan.md` §20. Summarised here because the sealed-content design
depends on both.

1. **An item's kind is fixed at first publication** (withdrawal and its reversal aside).
   A publisher MUST NOT change an item between fragment and thread; a reader that sees it
   happen treats it as it treats a stealth edit (§13.3). Reason: a fragment is a promise
   to whoever quotes it — one layer, one origin's words, bounded size. If it could become
   a thread, a quoter's next republish would pull in nested third-party content it never
   chose, which the quoter's provenance list does not record; under Level 3 that content
   could include sealed entries under other locks. A fragment that needs to grow is
   quoted by a new thread, which is the protocol's own way of composing. Thread to
   fragment is refused by the same rule for simplicity.
   *Evidence:* the studio's editor offers the switch only on drafts; in the session-64
   crawl cache none of the 8 identities held at more than one version changes kind (a
   small sample); and the studio's API refuses the change on a published item
   (`src/api.ts`, `updateItemOperation`: "kind is fixed once an item has been
   published"). So the rule writes down what the reference client already does.
2. **Machine-made items are threads.** An item the client creates on the author's behalf
   (a tangled span, any future automatic split) is a thread, since "fragment" is the
   author's own statement that this is a leaf. **`tangling-plan.md` was revised the same
   session**: parts are threads, and the two refusals that kept them legal fragments (a
   span containing a quote, a span over 1,000 characters) are gone. The reader's `burst`
   rule in `background-plan.md` was widened from "a fragment" to "an item"; it was not yet
   built. Spec §13.7's rule is already kind-neutral ("items … named in its
   `transclusions[]`"); only its closing phrase says "its fragments", a wording fix for
   the same revision.

Also for tangling: a span tangled inside a sealed region inherits the enclosing lock by
default, or tangling would publish sealed text in the clear.

## 7. Identity

The design authenticates **origins**, as Webmention verification does. Which person an
origin belongs to is the approver's own knowledge (a roster, sign-on evidence, an
h-card).

It is meant to sit inside #11 under two conditions, which the RFC must state and Fable
must rule:

- **One key per origin, in the manifest, never inside `author`.** A per-author key would
  make people addressable.
- **Encryption only.** No signing or authorship meaning; that stays with the reserved
  identity work.

The key belongs in the manifest because other clients read it to write grants, and page
markup cannot carry a claim that crosses clients (#41). An h-card may mirror it.

**Shared origins.** Where several people publish from one host, a grant reaches the
origin. Who inside may read it is the house's business, run off the wire as comments are
(tn-7). The house can read everything granted to the origin; a member who cannot accept
that needs their own origin. A person who only reads still needs a minimal origin to hold
a key.

*Not re-read this session:* the identity note's gate (#35) and tn-3 on groups. Opus
checks both against this section while drafting.

## 8. Room for RFC-7 (payments)

Payment supervenes on permission: it is one more ground on which a home grants a key, and
a paid-up lock is an ordinary lock. On the wire there is one dimension, keys. RFC-6 must
therefore keep five things true:

1. Requests are plain HTTP to one URL per lock and per entry, so a 402 challenge can be a
   fourth answer.
2. The live answer (the key in the response) is a first-class path and the recipient
   need not be an origin, since a payer may be an anonymous wallet or an agent.
3. A lock's home is a URL, not necessarily the origin.
4. Entry keys are per version, so "this pinned version" is a unit that can be sold.
5. No price and no rail appears in any protocol document.

The mapping RFC-7 will start from: a subscription is a grant of a lock key; buying one
item or version is a grant of one entry key; a period ending is lock rotation; whether
the archive is included is whether each new lock is granted the previous one.

**Refused in advance, for both RFCs:** any restriction on use after delivery (expiring
rentals, read counts, metering). The design expresses "who gets which key, when" and
nothing else.

## 9. What the RFC must settle or ask

Each is open. The RFC states the options and a proposal; Fable rules at the edit or at
the close. R4's table (`plans/l3/r4-spec-audit.md`) is the checklist for the first group.

**Wire shape**
1. **A second hash.** An edit to sealed text alone leaves `content_hash` unchanged, so
   Level 2 readers will file it as maintenance (§13.2). Holders need a sealed hash of
   what they read; say what maintenance means for each audience.
2. **Key commitment.** AES-GCM does not bind a key to a ciphertext, so one entry could
   show two readers two texts, and a later reveal would prove nothing. Propose a
   commitment.
3. **Algorithms.** The intersection of browsers, Workers, Go and Python's `cryptography`
   package is AES-GCM, HKDF-SHA-256 and X25519. Python's standard library has no cipher,
   so the stdlib rule (session 29) cannot hold for decryption; say what replaces it.
   Confirm the Workers runtime, which R2 did not cover.
4. **Padding.** Short spans leak their length. Propose bucket sizes.
5. **Locating a span when rendering from `content_md`.** The baked class serves clients
   that render `content_html`; a markdown-rendering client may need a text selector as
   partial quotes have (§10.3).
6. **What stays clear.** Proposed starting point: references (`transclusions[]`,
   `links[]`, `stub_of`) and counters clear, words sealed. Then changelog notes, `author`,
   `media` and its files, `generated[]`, each by R4's rows.
7. **Lock ids are visible**, so strangers can see which entries share a lock. Accept, or
   blind them.

**Surface**
8. **The index and the feed.** Under this design sealed items appear in both with their
   covers, and spans add no events. Confirm against §6.2 and §7, and say what a plain RSS
   reader sees.
9. **Pins.** A sealed version may be pinned; the promise is ciphertext. Say whether the
   protocol says anything about keeping keys.
10. **The carve-out.** The request endpoint is a second dynamic surface (#1, #13), and
    the home URL sits in the entry, not the manifest, to stay clear of #52. Check that
    reading of #52.
11. **Grant path derivation**, and whether hosts that list directories or publish their
    repository defeat it.
12. **Abuse of the request endpoint**: rate limits, and whether every request gets the
    same first answer so the endpoint is not an oracle.

**Behaviour**
13. **Mentions.** Can a sealed item send a verifiable mention (§15.4), given clear
    references?
14. **Generation.** Sealed text fed to a generator comes out in the generating item's
    audience (§5.7). Client policy, as #93 F3 treated quoting across a boundary?
15. **Revocation as a retention event.** Does a conforming reader drop decrypted copies
    when it loses access, as on withdrawal (§13.4)?
16. **Post-quantum.** Grant files wrap keys to classical public keys. Disclose, or
    require a hybrid.
17. **Conformance.** Which clauses change, and whether the live crawler needs to know
    Level 3 before any node emits a sealed entry.

**Questions for implementers** (the RFC's own §, as RFC-5 §7): whether readers without
their own origin are a real population; whether one home per organisation or one per node
is what they would run; what evidence their ladder would want; whether inline redaction
as covers is enough or they need more.
